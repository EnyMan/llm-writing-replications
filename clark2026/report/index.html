Why is AI-generated language so hard to understand? A replication

Clark, Martin and Mahowald (2026) measure eight features linked to reading difficulty in AI-written text and compare them with human writing. Their data was not released, so we rebuilt it from the paper's appendix. In the essay setting, on — of — model-and-measure checks our models fall on the same side of the student essays as the paper's, in chat on — of —, and in coding on — of — checks where the paper draws a human line. The clear exception is the paper's finding that every model overuses inanimate subjects with animate verbs: in our data that depends on the model and the setting.

Essays: our values next to the paper's

Each model wrote ten essays for each of seven PERSUADE 2.0 assignments, the human baseline is the 2,934 top-scoring student essays for the same assignments, and Wikipedia is the n-gram reference. A filled dot is our value with its 95% interval (essays resampled within assignment), the open diamond is the paper's value read off its figure, and the dashed line is the student essays as we measure them.

We also gave the same assignments to seven models the paper did not test. Hemmingway-1, a finetune of Qwen 3.8 27B marketed as writing like a person, moves its base model toward the student essays on most measures: reduced object relatives, dependency length, stacked nouns and denominal verbs all land closer to the human line. Its one exception is inanimate subjects with animate verbs, where it rises. The Antislop finetune of the same base, made to avoid formulaic prose, has the highest rate of inanimate subjects of any model here. Mistral Small 3.1 stays closest to the student essays on the n-gram measures.

Chat: our values next to the paper's

Each model held ten twelve-turn conversations, one per opening message from the paper, with a simulated user played by Claude Sonnet 4.6. Only the model's replies are measured. The human line is a 500,000-word sample of OpenSubtitles, and the rest of OpenSubtitles is the n-gram reference. The paper draws a human line in chat only for the object relatives, dependency length and stacked nouns.

Denominal verbs replicate closely in chat even though the classifier's thresholds were set on the essays, and dependency length lands within a few hundredths of the paper for every model. Inanimate subjects flip for Luna: in our chats it uses them about as often as Kimi, while the paper has it near Llama, the reverse of what we found in essays. Every model, ours and the paper's, uses far more never-seen word pairs than subtitle dialogue does.

Coding: our values next to the paper's

Each model ran up to five sessions in the Pi coding agent, one per small open-source Python repository, each in its own container. A session is an opening bug-sweep request followed by up to ten follow-ups from a simulated repository owner (Claude Sonnet 4.6), and it ends early once the model's replies reach about 8,000 tokens. Only the model's final reply in each turn is measured. The human line is a 500,000-word sample of StackExchange questions and answers (Stack Overflow, Software Engineering, Code Review and Unix), and the rest of the downloaded StackExchange prose (484 million words) is the n-gram reference. Besides the paper's three open models we ran six of the essay and chat additions, Hemmingway-1 locally; Antislop and Gembrain have four sessions each, and Mistral Small 3.1 is left out (see below).

Our StackExchange sample lands almost exactly on the paper's human line for both kinds of object relative and for dependency length, so the human side of this setting replicates well. In coding, the paper's headline finding holds for every model we ran: all of them use inanimate subjects with animate verbs ("the parser treats", "the test expects") more often than StackExchange writers, from Kimi at 27 per 1,000 sentences to the Antislop finetune at 86. Luna stacks the most nouns, as in the paper. Most models use two to four times more never-seen word pairs than the human text; the Antislop and Gembrain finetunes come closest to StackExchange on both n-gram measures. Samples are small, 3,000 to 16,000 words per model (Antislop's 198 sentences are the fewest), so the intervals are wide. Hemmingway-1 reverses its essay result here: next to its Qwen 3.8 27B base it uses more denominal verbs (107 against 71 per 1,000 sentences), stacks more nouns and uses more never-seen word pairs, moving away from StackExchange; it is closer to the human text only on inanimate subjects and reduced object relatives. Programming verbs such as allow, require, show and promise were never counted as needing an animate subject; "expects" was, rarely, in both the human and the model text.

What we rebuilt, and where we had to choose
  • Human essays: PERSUADE 2.0, independent assignments, holistic score 5 or 6, missing grade, ELL status or score dropped. Train and test sets together give exactly the paper's 2,934 essays across seven assignments.
  • Model essays: the paper's system prompt ("You are {assistant}, an AI assistant made by {vendor}.") with the assignment as the user message, reasoning at medium, provider defaults otherwise. Ten essays per assignment.
  • Parsing: spaCy en_core_web_trf on GPU, sentences split with syntok, as in the paper. Dependency length is standardized to Wikipedia's sentence-length mix; counting punctuation positions in the distance is what reproduces the paper's values to within ±0.04.
  • Object relatives: excluded when-, where- and why-relatives, relatives with a stranded preposition, infinitival relatives and "the reason they…" heads, which the parser otherwise counts as reduced object relatives.
  • N-grams: a KenLM trigram on all of English Wikipedia (2.88 billion words), as the paper's values imply; smaller references give rates five to twenty times too high.
  • Denominal verbs and inanimate subjects: the paper judged parser candidates with Jev 1.13 but did not publish its questions. We used Torchcast Decision 4B locally with our own definitional questions, refined on explanations from Qwen3.5-4B, and set each acceptance threshold so the student essays match the paper's human rate (0.35 and 0.71). Candidates exclude passives and participles used as adjectives.
  • Chat: the paper's system prompt, its ten opening messages and its simulated-user rules, with Claude Sonnet 4.6 as the user (reasoning off, prompt caching on). The paper does not publish the user personas, so we wrote one short neutral persona per topic. Conversations stop at twelve model replies; no fallback message was needed. Replies are cleaned as the appendix describes: code blocks removed, inline code and links replaced by a placeholder, no sentence across a heading, list item or table cell.
  • Chat references: OpenSubtitles v2018 English. A 500,000-word sample drawn across the whole file is the human corpus; a KenLM trigram on the rest of it (2.43 billion words, sample held out) is the n-gram reference. Scoring the sample against a reference that contains it put its never-seen pairs at 14 per 1,000 instead of 19. The denominal-verb candidates use the sample's noun and verb counts, and the classifier thresholds are the ones set on the essays.
  • Coding: Pi 0.84.4, the paper's harness, one Docker container per session, the repositories pinned to fixed commits. The opening message tells the agent the box has no network access, but unlike the paper's sandbox (which could reach only an API proxy) our containers have normal network access, since the agent has to reach the model provider. Models ran through NanoGPT, except GLM 5, Qwen 3.8 27B and the simulated user, which ran through OpenRouter: through NanoGPT, GLM 5 wrote its tool calls as plain text, which ended its turns before any tool ran, so those sessions were discarded. Reasoning matches the essay and chat runs (medium for the paper's models, low for GLM 5.3 Flash, off otherwise). Hemmingway-1 ran locally in Ollama (Q4_K_M) with a 96k-token context and an 8-bit KV cache; at 64k its longest sessions ran out of context mid tool call, so those runs were discarded. A turn's reply is the model's last message when it makes no further tool call; a turn that ends on a tool call has no reply, and the next user message is then a plain "ok, keep going". The human corpus is a seeded sample of whole StackExchange threads (question and answers, comments and code removed); the n-gram reference is a KenLM trigram on every other thread's prose. The classifier thresholds are the ones set on the essays.
  • Discarded coding sessions: Mistral Small 3.1 fell into edit loops (the same edits resent over and over) in four of five sessions and is left out. Gembrain looped once and Antislop's stencil session failed (a malformed tool call, then provider overloads), so each has four sessions. Provider errors and timeouts were retried; a retried turn restarts from the session state before it.
  • Stacked nouns count common nouns only. Counting proper nouns too, subtitle name chains put the human chat rate at 1.85 per 1,000 words against the paper's 0.40.
  • The paper's values come from its vector figure: each plotted point is mapped back through the axis ticks.
